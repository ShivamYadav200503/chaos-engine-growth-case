2. About the games

Game A is a casual merge puzzle game, available on Android and iPhone around the world. It makes money in two ways:

  • Ads inside the game. Players watch video ads (sometimes in exchange for a reward), and see full-screen ads and small banners.
  • In-app purchases (IAP). Some players pay real money for items or progress.

Game B is a casual tile-matching puzzle game. It earns almost all its money from ads.

We find new players for both games in two ways:

  • Paid advertising. We pay ad networks to show our ads in other apps. A person sees an ad, installs the game and starts playing.
    • Game A buys on four networks: Net-A, Net-B, Net-C and Net-D.
    • Game B has used those four and two more: Net-E and Net-F.
    The networks are described in Appendix B.
  • Organic. People find the game by themselves, for example by searching the app store.

New to this world? Every term is explained where it first matters, and in the glossary. You do not need prior advertising experience.

Question 1 · about 3–4 hoursWhat is a new player worth, in Game A and in Game B?

The question

For each game, and for every network and country (or group of countries) in its data, how much money does a newly acquired player bring in compared with what they cost? Answer at two points in time:

  • by day 120 after install;
  • after one year (365 days).

Do those players pay back their cost within 120 days, only within a year, or not at all?

The data contains errors. Find them, flag them, and make sure your predictions handle them properly. Don't just delete what looks odd; decide what each problem means for the numbers.

Then test whether your approach carries from one game to the other:

  1. Build a model on Game A's data and predict Game A.
  2. Apply that same Game A model to Game B.
  3. Build a new model on Game B's data and predict Game B.
  4. Apply the Game B model to Game A.

Compare the results. Where do they differ, by how much, and why? What does this tell us about using one game's model for another game?

Every spending decision rests on these numbers. If the data behind them is wrong, or the model doesn't fit the game, the plan built on it will be wrong too.

Key idea 1: the same players, counted by different sources

Three kinds of source describe the same players:

  • The ad network: what it charged us, and the installs it believes it caused.
  • Our measurement partner (MMP): a neutral tool inside the game that decides which network, if any, gets credit for each install, and tracks what those players earn. This is our standard for installs and player value.
  • The game's own tracking ("telemetry"): sees everything players do in the game, but not which ad brought them in.

These sources count in different ways, so their numbers will not match exactly. Before you trust a number, know which source it came from and how that source defines it.

Example: a network reports 1,200 installs yesterday; the MMP credits it with 1,000. Both can be right: the network counts installs that another network also claims. The MMP gives each install to only one network.

A cohort is the group of players who installed on the same day from the same source. A cohort's "day-30" figure can only exist once the cohort is at least 30 days old.

Key idea 2: paying back the cost
  • CPI (cost per install) = what we spent ÷ number of installs.
  • ROAS at day N (return on ad spend) = the money a cohort has brought in by day N ÷ what that cohort cost. A cohort pays back when its ROAS reaches 100%.
Example: Cohort X cost $4.00 per install and brings in $4.40 per player by day 120, so it pays back (110%). Cohort Y cost only $0.50 per install but brings in $0.40, so it never pays back (80%). A cheap install is not automatically a good install, and an expensive one is not automatically bad. What matters is how much a player brings in compared with what they cost.
Key idea 3: always look country by country

Countries differ a lot: installs can cost ten times more in one country than in another. When a network starts or stops buying in a country, its overall numbers move even if nothing else changed.

Example: a network buys only in the US at $4.00 per install. Then it adds a cheap country at $0.50. The network's overall cost per install drops by two-thirds, yet no country got cheaper. Only the mix changed.

That's why we work by cell: one network, on one platform, in one market. A market is a country or a group of countries.

Key idea 4: predicting value, near and far

LTV (lifetime value) up to day N is the total money an average player has brought in by day N. Money keeps arriving for months after install, more slowly as time goes on:

The 1-year value is harder: no cohort in our data is followed for more than 180 days, so part of every year must be estimated. Long-range estimates can be far off, which makes it important to be honest about how sure you are.

A model here is simply your method for turning what you can see today into those predictions. We don't prescribe one. We will judge yours on four things:

  • it only uses information you would really have today;
  • you have checked how accurate it is on players whose outcome is already known;
  • it gives honest ranges;
  • someone else could re-run it and get the same answer.
What to do
  1. Check the data first, for both games. Find the errors and anything misleading. For each problem:
    • say what it is and show the evidence in numbers;
    • say which figures it would distort;
    • say how you handled it in your predictions: corrected, excluded, or kept on purpose.
  2. Choose your cells for each game (network × platform × country or country group) and explain how you grouped countries.
  3. For each cell, show:
    • recent weekly spend (the average over 1–28 September, which we call the baseline; for networks we have stopped, say when they stopped);
    • cost per install;
    • ROAS at day 7 and day 30, from cohorts you consider reliable.
  4. Build your Game A model and predict, for each Game A cell and as ranges:
    • ROAS at day 120;
    • 1-year LTV and 1-year ROAS.
    Also give totals by network and by country group. Show how accurate the model is where the outcome is known, and explain how you estimated the part of the year the data can't show.
  5. Apply the Game A model unchanged to Game B and record the predictions.
  6. Build a Game B model from Game B's data, predict Game B, and then apply it to Game A.
  7. Compare all four sets:
    • A on A;
    • A on B;
    • B on B;
    • B on A.
    Where are the differences biggest? What causes them? Which parts of a model can be shared between games, and which must be rebuilt for each game?
  8. For each game, sort the cells into three groups:
    • those that pay back within 120 days;
    • those that pay back only within a year;
    • those that don't pay back even in a year.
  9. Fill in Template_predictions.csv (Game A only): eight predictions, each with a range you are 80% confident in. We will score them against the actual outcomes, both for accuracy and for whether your ranges were honest.

Hand in, in the appendix:

  • a data problems table: problem · where · evidence · what it distorts · how you handled it;
  • a value table for each game: cells, plus totals by network and by country, with day-120 and 1-year figures;

Question 2 · about 2 hours · most importantHow should we spend Game A's budget next quarter?

The question

Give us one spend plan for Game A, from 6 October to 28 December. It must:

  • meet the CFO's rule;
  • within that rule, give the CEO as much growth as possible.

Plan only for the networks and countries we already buy in; don't add new countries. Show how you would split the quarter's budget across networks and countries. Tell us:

  • what it should deliver;
  • how you balanced profit (CFO) against growth (CEO);
  • what could go wrong.

Say whether you counted organic players towards the CFO's rule, and why.

This is the decision the CEO and CFO are waiting for. Use your values from Question 1 and the rules in Appendix A.

Key idea: the next dollar earns less than the average dollar

The first dollars you spend reach the people most likely to install and play. Spend more, and the network has to reach people who are harder to convince and often play less. So each install tends to cost more, and each new player tends to be worth less. That's why the average return can hide what the next dollar brings back.

Example: a cell earns $11,000 by day 120 from $10,000 of spend (110%). Double the spend to $20,000 and it earns $16,700. The average is still a respectable 83%, but the extra $10,000 only brought in $5,700 (57%).

The team's working assumptions about how strongly each network behaves like this are in Appendix A.

What to do
  1. For each network, platform and country, set the total spend for the quarter and its share of the budget, following every rule in Appendix A. You may group small countries, but name the countries in each group. Also give totals by network and by country.
  2. Give the result you expect for the quarter's players, each as a range:
    • installs;
    • money and ROAS by day 120;
    • money and ROAS after one year.
  3. Explain how you balanced the CFO and the CEO: how much growth you gave up for profit, or the other way round, and which rules limit the plan.
  4. State whether you counted organic players, and what in the data supports your choice.
  5. Where a cell misses the 120-day rule but pays back within a year, say what you did with it and why.
  6. Show at least one place where you gave more money to a cell with a higher cost per install, or explain why you didn't.
  7. Assume each cell gets enough fresh creatives to hold its baseline CPI. Question 3 works out what that takes.
  8. Name the biggest risk to the plan.

Hand in:

  • In the memo: the plan and its expected result.
  • In the appendix: the plan table (by network and country, with totals) and your reasoning.
  • The plan in Template_plan.csv.

Question 3 · about 1–1.5 hoursWhat ads should we build for each network next quarter?

The question

Using Game A's ad-level data and the wear-out rule in Appendix A, tell us:

  • what kinds of ads to build for each network, and why;
  • how many new creatives each network needs next quarter to carry your spend plan from Question 2, and when they need to be ready;
  • a creative plan for the quarter that puts this together.
Key idea 1: what gets installs is not always what gets value

An ad is called a creative. It can be a video, a playable (a small interactive demo of the game), an image, or a combination. Networks show more often the ads that turn views into installs cheaply. The usual measure is IPM, installs per 1,000 ad views. A high IPM makes installs cheaper, but it says nothing about how valuable those players are.

Example: Ad P gets 30 installs per 1,000 views, so each install costs $2.00, but players bring in only $0.50 in their first week (25% back). Ad Q gets 12 installs per 1,000 views, so each install costs $5.00, but players bring in $2.25 in their first week (45% back). The network may prefer P. Q is the better business.

Networks also differ in what kinds of ads they accept and how much detail they report (Appendix B).

Key idea 2: creatives wear out, so volume follows spend

The more often people see the same ad, the less they respond, so its CPI creeps up. More spend means more impressions, and more impressions means creatives wear out faster. That's why a bigger budget needs more new creatives, delivered on time.

Example: a creative starts at $2.00 per install and gets 10% more expensive for every million impressions. It is retired at $2.60 (+30%), so it lasts 3 million impressions. A cell that will serve 12 million impressions in the quarter needs about four creatives' worth of life. If the plan doubles that cell's spend, it needs about eight.
What to do
  1. For each network, show what the data says: which types of ad get the most spend, and which bring in the most valuable players.
  2. From your Question 2 plan, work out how many impressions each network will serve next quarter. Then use the wear-out rule to work out how many new creatives it needs and by when, remembering that today's creatives only carry the first two weeks.
  3. Recommend what to build for each network: the type of ad, how many, and the evidence behind each choice.
  4. Lay out the creative plan for the quarter: what gets built, for which network, and ready by which week.
  5. Say what happens to CPI and to your plan's results if creatives arrive late or you build too few.

Hand in: one page in the appendix, including a table: network · impressions next quarter · new creatives needed · ready by · type of ad.

Creatives wear out

An ad gets less effective the more it is shown. For planning, use this rule:

  • A new creative starts at the cell's baseline CPI.
  • For every 1 million impressions a creative has served, its CPI rises by the percentage in the table (in a straight line).
  • When its CPI is 30% above where it started, it is retired and stops serving.
  • A network spreads a cell's impressions evenly across the creatives that are live.
  • Impressions follow spend: impressions = spend ÷ CPM × 1,000, using each cell's baseline CPM. You can calculate CPM from GameA_ua_daily.csv.
  • Assume today's creatives are nearly worn out: they carry the first two weeks of the quarter, and after that every impression must come from new creatives.
  • If a network runs out of fresh creatives, its CPI keeps rising at the table's rate. As long as you replace creatives in time, assume the cell keeps its baseline CPI.
  • There is no limit on how many creatives the team can build. Your job is to work out how many are needed, for which network, and when.
